What Is Suita? How to Read the "Layers" of a Japanese Natural Whetstone

Summary

Suita refers to stones taken from a layer of natural whetstone that contains small holes — "su" (nests). A nest is not a defect in itself; it is the character of that layer, and small, shallow nests have little effect on sharpening. A layer name is a useful clue to a stone's likely hardness, but the final judgement always has to be made stone by stone.

What a "layer" means

A natural whetstone is cut from strata inside a mountain. Each stratum is called a "layer" (sō), and hardness, colour and pattern change with depth and position. Even from the same mountain, the layer a stone came from can change how it sharpens a great deal — which is why, alongside the mine name, the layer name has become an important clue to a stone's character. How layers are divided and named differs by mountain; what follows is the range generally known for finishing stones of the Higashiyama (Kyoto) system.

The main layers, and what they tend to mean

LayerWhat it meansHardness tendency (a guide only)
Hon-suitaThe part of the suita layer regarded as especially fine-grained and high qualityMostly medium to somewhat hard
SuitaThe general term for layers whose surface shows nests (small holes)Wide range, from fairly soft (releases slurry easily) to hard
Tenjō-suita ("ceiling suita")Known as stone from the upper (ceiling) side of the suita layerClose to suita in tendency
TomaeOften stone from near a layer boundary; pattern tends to showMostly medium
UchigumoriKnown as a layer long used for building the hazy kasumi finish on a blade's flatMostly relatively soft
RengeStones showing a lotus-flower-like mottled pattern on the surfaceVaries greatly by stone
Karasu ("crow")Stones showing dark, crow-feather-like mottlingMostly hard, for finishing

Hardness in this table is a tendency only. The same layer name can mean different actual hardness depending on the mountain and the exact position it was taken from. Renge and karasu are also not strictly layer names — they describe how a pattern appears on the surface. That looseness in the terminology is part of what makes natural-stone vocabulary confusing, and interesting.

Reading the nests, and how they affect sharpening

Good nests, and nests to watch

  • Small, shallow nests: part of suita's character. They can help hold sharpening slurry on the surface and usually cause little trouble in normal sharpening.
  • Deep, large nests: a fine edge can dip into them, so they are best avoided for fine finishing work.
  • Not to be confused with toxic veins (dokusuji): a hard vein that can damage an edge is a different thing from a nest. A shiny line running across the stone, or a catching feeling while sharpening, calls for caution.

How nests affect sharpening

A nest does make the stone surface less than perfectly uniform. In return, slurry (tonoko) stays on the surface more easily, and for a kasumi finish — where the flat of the blade is deliberately clouded — that is often welcomed. How much it matters depends on the nest's depth, size and distribution, and on the width of the edge being sharpened: a nest that barely matters on a wide kitchen knife or plane blade can matter on a razor-fine edge. When buying, ask not only for photos but for a description in words of the nests' depth and whether any toxic veins are present.

Frequently asked questions

Is a suita "nest" a defect?
Not in itself. A nest is a feature of the suita layer, and small, shallow nests usually cause no trouble in sharpening. Deep, large nests, or veins that catch the edge (toxic veins), can get in the way — so depth and position are what to look at.
How is hon-suita different from suita?
In general usage, hon-suita is the name given to the part of the suita layer regarded as especially good. But sellers use the term with a range of meanings, so do not judge by the name alone — check the stone's actual properties, such as its stated hardness and test record.
If I know the layer name, do I know the hardness?
Only as a rough guide. The same layer varies in hardness by mountain and position. Treat the layer name as reference information, and confirm the individual stone by its hardness indication and test record.
What blades is suita suited to?
Because it releases slurry easily and builds a blade's flat well, it is often used for the kasumi finish on Japanese kitchen knives. But the range is wide — hard suita leans toward final finishing, soft suita toward mid-finishing — so choose hardness to match the job.
